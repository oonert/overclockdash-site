THE ENGINE, EXPLAINED

A Bluetooth adapter in the truck's diagnostic port, and the Field Computer reads everything the engine computer knows. Every cheap dongle shows you numbers. This one tells you what they mean.

  • Live engine gauges. Speed, rpm, coolant, intake air, load, throttle, timing, airflow, fuel trims and more, around fifty readings.
  • Check engine codes in plain words. Every code a 1996 to 2002 truck can set, with a name, what it means, and what to check first.
  • Ranked by the drive home. Stop engine, stop soon, use caution, get checked, or just emissions. Only the serious ones take the screen.
  • Pending codes. A fault the computer has seen once but not confirmed, caught before the light even comes on.
  • Snapshots. What the engine was doing the second each code set. A handheld reader plugged in later cannot know that.
  • Will it pass smog? The truck's self-tests as a bar of blocks, with a straight answer.
  • Clear codes, honestly. A guarded clear, and then the truth about what comes next.
  • Towing. Transmission and coolant temperature on a grade, with one verdict: normal, caution, warning, pull over.
  • Oil and fuel pressure. Add a sender and see the number all the time, not an idiot light.
  • True MPG. Fill-ups logged against real miles and real gallons, and the dash learns your truck's airflow sensor from the pump.

GPS, BUILT IN

A receiver inside, an antenna on the case. It works on every truck, whatever the year.

  • Speed from the sky. Ground speed from satellites, even on a truck that cannot report it.
  • The clock sets itself. Time and time zone from GPS. Drive across a line and the hour is right.
  • Every actual mile. Engine data and GPS counted together, each trusted only when it deserves to be, so a parked truck never gains a foot and a tunnel never loses one.
  • Altitude, climb and grade. Barometer and GPS together, with gain and loss since you left.
  • Compass heading. On the clock, on the gauges, wherever you want it.
  • Places and home. Save places, get a WELCOME HOME sign, and a sign when you cross a state line.
  • It notices the pump. Stop at a fuel station and the dash asks if you filled up. It learns where you buy fuel.
  • Road trip. Distance, time and fuel for the long one, day by day.

IT WRITES EVERYTHING DOWN

A silent flight recorder on a memory card in the dash. Pull the card and open it in any spreadsheet. No cloud, no account, no subscription, and none of it leaves the truck.

  • Every second of every drive. More than seventy readings a second: speed, rpm, every temperature, volts, position, altitude, fuel, tilt and the rest.
  • Every start. Resting volts, the crank dip, charging, the engine light and readiness, in a battery log and an engine log.
  • Every code. As it comes and goes, pending ones included, with what the engine was doing.
  • Every tank. Real gallons against real miles.
  • Every parked hour. With the key off the dash wakes every hour, or every 15 or 30 minutes, writes one line (battery, pressure, every probe, position, solar) and sleeps again. It will not flatten the battery: pick the volts where it stops.
  • A breadcrumb trail. A dot every twenty seconds, off with one switch that stops all position logging for good.
  • Room for years. An 8 GB card holds over ten thousand hours of driving. The card is checked at every start, so a dying card cannot log in silence.

WHAT IT WATCHES FOR

On whenever the key is on, watching the whole time. The things that cannot wait take the screen with a marching hazard border. Everything else stays out of your way.

  • Overheat. The real coolant number, not a factory gauge that sits dead centre until it is too late.
  • Over-voltage. First priority and instant: a failed regulator is cooking the battery.
  • Not charging. The alternator quit; the volts tell it before the lights dim.
  • Serious codes. Stop engine and stop soon take the screen, in plain words. Lesser codes only light the small lamp.
  • Low oil pressure. With a sender fitted, the one alert that says stop the engine now.
  • Fridge, freezer, storm, towing. Each with its own switch.
  • Every alert fails to off. A sensor that goes quiet disarms its alert instead of crying wolf.

BUILT FOR THE TRUCK

Not a phone holder and not a kit. A purpose-made unit that fits the slot the factory clock came out of.

  • A fully custom circuit board. Designed for this job, nothing bolted together from modules.
  • Two processors. One runs the screen, sensors and logging. The second does nothing but Bluetooth, so the screen never stutters.
  • A screen made for the truck. 2 inch for the 4Runner, 2.8 inch for the Tacoma, behind a bezel made for each dash.
  • Protected. Its own fuse, surge protection against the spikes trucks make, reverse-polarity protection, and a graceful fifteen-second shutdown when the key goes off.
  • Expandable. Screw terminals for four temperature probes and two senders, and a powered expansion port for add-ons.
  • Nothing to wear out. No USB port, no coin cell. Updates from WiFi or the card; GPS puts the time back after a battery disconnect.
  • Made in Vancouver, Washington. By someone who drives one.

WHAT IT WILL NOT DO

Trust is built here, not in the feature list.

  • It does not tune or reprogram anything. It reads. The one thing it ever writes is clearing codes, and only when you arm it and hold.
  • It never guesses. If a sensor has not answered, the screen says so instead of inventing a number.
  • No cloud, no account, no subscription. Your data lives on a card in your dash.
  • No position logging unless you want it. One switch, and the column in the file stays empty.
  • No live engine data before 1996. Those trucks have nothing to read. They get everything else, and the engine pages simply are not there.
